01 — About the program

A small collection, built back up on purpose.

Gootyland is a home breeding program, not a warehouse operation. The focus is quality of care over volume — which at times has meant deliberately holding numbers back rather than pushing them up.

The collection has produced up to roughly 1,000 slings in a single year. Today it's smaller and more deliberate: mostly older adult females, alongside a newer generation of juveniles around 15–18 months old, as numbers are rebuilt with care.

  • 01
    Low-stress handling Every interaction with the animals is designed to minimize stress, from feeding to rehousing to any venom collection.
  • 02
    Ethical harvesting — in development A low-stress venom collection method doesn't exist yet in a form that puts the animal's welfare first. Building one is the next project.
  • 03
    Education first Husbandry knowledge is shared openly, so more keepers can raise healthy, unstressed animals.

03 — Tarantula education

Where "Old World" and "New World" even come from.

It's not a tarantula-specific term — it's borrowed straight from geography. "Old World" refers to Europe, Africa, and Asia, the parts of the globe known to Europeans before 1492. "New World" refers to the Americas, named that once Columbus's voyages made Europeans aware the continent existed.

Tarantula keepers use the same split because it happens to line up perfectly with biology: African and Asian species fall on the Old World side, American species fall on the New World side — and because those two landmasses evolved their tarantulas in isolation from each other, each side developed a completely different defense strategy. Old World species leaned into speed and venom; New World species leaned into urticating hairs and a more cautious temperament.

04 — Red-listed species

P. metallica isn't just rare in the hobby — it's rare, period.

Poecilotheria metallica is classified as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List. It's endemic to a single small forest region in Andhra Pradesh, India — its entire known wild range is under 100 km². It's also listed under CITES Appendix II, which regulates its international trade.

The two biggest threats to the wild population are habitat loss from deforestation and firewood collection, and illegal collection for the pet trade — wild specimens taken and smuggled out to meet demand from keepers abroad.

Every P. metallica bred here started as a captive-bred egg sac, not a wild-caught animal. Local, legal captive breeding is the direct alternative to poaching: it lets demand for this species get met without a single spider being pulled from its last remaining forest.

05 — Kids & tarantulas

Curious kids and tarantulas can mix — with the right homework first.

A tarantula can be a genuinely great way to get a child excited about animals, patience, and careful observation. But "which species" matters as much as "how old is my kid" — the two major tarantula groups behave very differently, and mixing that up is where people get hurt.

  • 01
    Old World vs. New World isn't cosmetic Old World species (like our own P. metallica) tend to be faster, more defensive, and generally carry more medically significant venom. New World species are generally more docile, slower to bite, and typically milder if they do — though they defend themselves with irritating urticating hairs instead.
  • 02
    Research the exact species — not just "tarantula" Temperament and venom potency vary a lot even within these two groups. Before buying or bringing one home, look up that specific species' behavior, care needs, and bite reports — not just general tarantula facts.
  • 03
    Watch, don't just read Find responsible, experienced keepers on YouTube who show real handling, feeding, and enclosure setup — seeing calm, correct technique in action teaches a lot more than a care sheet alone.

The best beginner-and-kid setup usually isn't a bite risk at all — it's a calm, docile New World species kept in a well-built enclosure, observed rather than handled, with an adult always guiding the research first.

06 — The bigger mission

Venom is medicine waiting to be understood.

Tarantula venom is a complex cocktail of peptides, and researchers have already found compounds in various species' venom with potential relevance to pain and disease research. Right now, there isn't a good way to collect it without stressing or harming the animal — so that's the actual project: inventing a genuinely low-stress collection method, from the ground up.

Part of getting there is education, not just invention: understanding venom starts with understanding the animals producing it — their biology, their stress responses, and what "low-stress" actually means at a physiological level, not just a marketing phrase.

The goal isn't to breed more tarantulas. It's to build the thing that doesn't exist yet — a way to get venom to the people who need it without the animal ever paying the price for it.
